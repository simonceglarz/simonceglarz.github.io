Self-balancing robot

A two-wheeled inverted pendulum built around an ESP32. It balances on a cascaded PID loop, drives from a wireless joystick, and tunes live from a phone. I learned soldering, KiCad and SolidWorks on this project.

Status, October 2026: version 1 has balanced and driven since September. The custom PCB that replaces the perfboard is assembled and its power rails and I²C buses check out; the motor test on the new board is next.

Control

The inner loop is a PID on tilt angle. The angle comes from a complementary filter (α = 0.98: mostly integrated gyro, corrected slowly by the accelerometer), with the gyro bias calibrated at boot. The outer loop is a PI on averaged wheel velocity from the encoders, and it shifts the angle setpoint so the robot doesn’t creep across the floor while balanced.

  • The time step is measured each iteration and clamped at 20 ms, so a stall can’t produce a huge integral or derivative kick.
  • The derivative acts on the measurement, not the error, so changing the setpoint doesn’t spike the output.
  • Drive and steering commands are slew-limited (12 °/s of tilt trim, 80 units/s of steering), which stopped the robot tipping on sudden joystick moves.
  • Fall detection zeroes the motors and resets the integrators; the cutoff angle is tunable live.

Debugging notes

The fix that mattered most

DC gearmotors don’t move at all below a certain PWM duty, so the firmware adds a minimum output. My first version snapped any small command straight to that floor. Watching the output on the OLED, I saw it pinned at the floor for every error under about 3.4°, which turned the controller into bang-bang control right where it needed to be gentle. I replaced the snap with a proportional rescale per motor, so small errors still produce small differences in output. It was the biggest single improvement in stability.

A short the multimeter didn’t beep for

One encoder channel sat at mid-rail and its counts bounced. Continuity mode stayed silent, but measuring resistance showed 150–200 Ω to ground, above the meter’s beep threshold. On signal lines I now read the resistance instead of trusting the beeper.

Motor pinout by measurement

The seller’s wire colors didn’t follow the usual convention. Measuring about 8 Ω across red and white identified the winding. Going by color alone would have put battery voltage into the encoder’s Hall sensor.

Motors that twitched on USB power

With only USB connected, GPIO high states back-fed the DRV8833’s motor supply through its internal clamp diodes and the motors twitched. The rule now is battery on first and off last, and it is printed on the PCB’s silkscreen.

Live tuning dashboard

The ESP32 hosts a WiFi access point with a web page served over WebSocket. Sliders and nudge buttons set every gain, calibration value and the fall cutoff while the robot runs, and telemetry streams back at about 20 Hz. When a phone connects, the page loads the values the robot is actually running. This replaced a reflash for every tuning change.

Custom PCB

Revision 1 is a 2-layer, 120 × 70 mm KiCad board that replaces the hand-wired perfboard. The ESP32 DevKit, motor driver and buck converter plug in as modules. It passed ERC and DRC with zero errors and was fabricated by JLCPCB.

  • Two I²C buses. A full OLED refresh takes about 23 ms at 400 kHz (calculated), longer than two control periods. The IMU now has its own bus, so the display can’t hold it up once the display moves into its own FreeRTOS task.
  • GPIO remap for routing. I reassigned eight pins (four motor PWM, four encoder) so both groups run from connector to ESP32 with no crossings.
  • Traces sized for current. Using IPC-2221 for 1 oz copper and about 10 °C rise: 1.5 mm for the battery, 1.0 mm for motor outputs, 0.5 mm for 5 V, 0.25 mm for signals.
  • Debug access. Headers break out all four motor PWM lines and all four filtered encoder lines, with test points on each rail and on the IMU bus.
  • Expansion header. Power, the second I²C bus, UART, I²S, two servo PWM lines and an ADC input, for a future daughterboard.

Caught before ordering

A pre-order check found four errors that would have cost a board spin. A custom DRC rule with no layer condition flooded the report with 499 silkscreen violations and hit KiCad’s per-type cap, which could have hidden real copper errors. A caliper misreading had the DevKit header rows at 23.5 mm instead of 22.86 mm. The motor driver rows were 13.35 mm instead of the 12.70 mm on Adafruit’s drawing. The XT60 footprint was the same gender as the battery’s own plug.

Bring-up so far

I powered the board with every module removed and added the buck, the DevKit and the driver one at a time, checking each rail before the next: 8.05 V on the battery rail after the protection diode and 3.28 V on the 3.3 V rail. A scan of both I²C buses found the IMU alone on one and the OLED alone on the other, and the firmware runs on the new pin map with the OLED tracking tilt. The motor and encoder test is next.

Chassis

The chassis is three PETG plates on brass standoff columns, co-designed in SolidWorks. The motors hang below the bottom plate to raise the center of mass, which makes the pendulum slower and easier to balance.

Known limits and what’s next

  • The OLED refresh runs in the same loop as the controller, so 100 Hz isn’t guaranteed. Next: measure the loop on a logic analyzer, then split the firmware into FreeRTOS tasks.
  • Encoders are decoded on one channel’s edges only (2×), which can miss counts at speed. Next: the ESP32’s PCNT hardware counter for full 4× decoding.
  • Tuning values live in RAM and reset on power-off.
  • Version 2: an enclosure-style chassis around the PCB, stronger battery mounting, bumpers, and time-of-flight sensors for obstacle detection.
