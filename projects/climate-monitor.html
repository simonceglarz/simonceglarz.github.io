Battery climate monitor

A battery-powered ESP32 node that measures temperature, humidity and pressure for indoor plants, sleeps between readings, and reports to a cloud dashboard over WiFi. It started on a breadboard and ended on permanent perfboard.

Where the sleep current went

The ESP32 itself draws microamps in deep sleep, so I expected a tiny number. I measured 7.3 mV across a 1.1 Ω shunt: 6.64 mA. The difference was the dev board, not the chip. Its USB-to-serial bridge, its own voltage regulator and an always-on power LED all stay powered while the ESP32 sleeps. Removing the LED brought the floor to 4.34 mA. Getting to microamps means leaving the dev board behind for a bare module or a low-power board, which is the next version.

A BME280 driver from the datasheet

Instead of a library, I wrote the driver from Bosch’s datasheet. The BME280 reports raw readings that only become temperature, humidity and pressure after you apply compensation equations using calibration constants burned into each chip at the factory. The driver reads those registers at startup and applies the equations. It also puts the sensor into sleep mode by writing its control register directly.

Testing

I ran the node continuously for over 36 hours with telemetry logging, then cold-soaked it at 2.2 °C to see how it handled cold battery sag, humidity near saturation, and WiFi retries from inside a shielded enclosure.

What I’d do next

  • Move to a bare ESP32 module or a low-quiescent board to reach microamp sleep.
  • Design a KiCad PCB for it.
  • Model a vented enclosure in SolidWorks, keeping the sensor thermally isolated from the board.
